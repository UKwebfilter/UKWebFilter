## 1. The Metropolitan Police opens a criminal investigation into David Walliams over alleged non-recent serious sexual offences, separate from claims published this week by ITV and the Guardian

The Metropolitan Police has launched a criminal investigation into the comedian and children's author David Walliams over reports of alleged serious sexual offences. In a statement on Friday, the force said it had "received a number of allegations of non-recent serious sexual offences involving a man now in his 50s", and that those who had come forward were being supported by specialist officers. It is police policy not to name people under investigation.

The Met said the allegations it is investigating are separate from those published this week by ITV News and the Guardian, but that detectives are also "proactively reviewing that reporting to establish whether there is any evidence of offences". The Guardian reported that some of the new allegations date back to the 2000s and that at least some were made to police this week. Police declined to give details of the alleged offences, saying this was to reduce the chance of complainants being identified.

The ITV and Guardian reports, published after Walliams lost a High Court bid to stop them, accused him of using his fame to prey on young women and teenagers. One woman says he sexually assaulted her when she was 15, which he strongly denies. Walliams, 55, has said he is "not a sexual predator" and has "not broken the law". Detective Superintendent Mike Cagney urged anyone who had been a victim of a sexual offence to come forward, "no matter when it happened".

The US Air Force says its security forces responded to "suspected unauthorised drone activity" over RAF Lakenheath in Suffolk on Friday evening. Residents of a nearby village reported hearing sirens at the base, the BBC said, and Sky News reported that US personnel were told to take shelter.

A Ministry of Defence spokesperson said it was "coordinating with all relevant parties", that US security personnel had responded and that the situation was now resolved. "We are working closely with them to establish the facts," the MoD added. The West Suffolk MP, Nick Timothy, said he had been told by the base that "there is no threat to the public", and encouraged people to report anything suspicious "given the raised threat level".

Lakenheath is home to the 48th Fighter Wing, with almost 7,000 active-duty personnel, making it the largest US fighter operation in Europe. The incident follows heightened security at US bases in the UK after an incident at RAF Fairford in September, in which two men have been charged. ## 4. Alan Milburn says young people should have a legal guarantee of a job, training or support, with benefits shifted towards employment help, days before his review of youth unemployment is published

Young people should have a legal guarantee of a job, training or employment support, and could lose benefits if they refuse it, Alan Milburn has told the BBC. The former Labour minister's review of youth unemployment and welfare, covering the almost one million young people not in education, employment or training, is due to be published on Tuesday.

In an interview to be broadcast on Sunday, Milburn said spending on benefits for young people is 25 times as much as spending on employment support, and argued the ratio should shift to two to one. "The pendulum's swung far too far from the one to the other," he said. That would mean significant cuts to benefit spending and extra taxpayers' money for training, education and support. He acknowledged some young people would lose out on benefits, arguing the system should focus on helping people into work.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham called the report a "watershed moment" with big implications for the welfare state, saying it pointed towards "guaranteeing support for young people" rather than leaving them on benefits into their twenties. But asked whether he would accept the recommendations in full, Burnham said: "I can't give you that blanket assurance." Milburn has warned his proposals will not be "pain-free", and the Work and Pensions Secretary is expected to respond next week.

## 5. Probation officers in England and Wales vote for industrial action over pay and workloads, starting with an overtime ban from 23 November, as the early release scheme adds to pressure

Probation officers in England and Wales have voted for industrial action over pay and workloads, in a blow to the government's prisoner early release scheme. Members of the union Napo rejected a 5% pay offer and backed overtime restrictions and other action short of a strike, likely to start on 23 November. The Guardian reports that plans are being made for strike action at a later stage, and the BBC said strikes could follow if the union deemed them necessary. The union had sought 12%.

Napo general secretary Ian Lawrence said the service was "already in crisis" before the early release scheme, which relies on probation staff to monitor freed prisoners in the community. He said the vote reflected "serious anger" about workloads and lost ground on pay compared with other prison and probation staff.

The scheme was introduced because prisons were dangerously close to capacity. About 700 eligible offenders were released on 1 October, with further groups of 650 on 13 October and 10 November, and the Guardian says about 2,500 offenders, including sex offenders, domestic abusers and stalkers, will be released by the end of the year. A Ministry of Justice spokesperson said the government had made a "strong offer", was recruiting 1,300 more officers and had "robust contingency plans" to keep offenders under supervision.

## 6. The FAA lifts the longest nationwide US ground stop since 9/11 after more than four hours, with thousands of flights cancelled and the transport secretary blaming overloaded routers in an ageing system

The US Federal Aviation Administration has lifted a nationwide ground stop on departing flights, the longest since the 11 September 2001 attacks, after a communication issue affected all air traffic control centres. The order was issued at 3:54pm Eastern time on Friday "due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety", and the airspace reopened at 8:15pm. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy warned that unwinding the disruption would be "slow".

Duffy blamed an "incredibly old" air traffic control system, saying routers "got overloaded with data" and produced inconsistencies in radar and communications data, so the system was shut down. He told reporters the network uses "1980s old copper lines" and pressed Congress to fund a new system. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no indication of an attack or a cyberattack and that the public was never in danger.

Counts of the disruption varied. The BBC, citing FlightAware, reported more than 8,500 delays and nearly 8,000 cancellations on Friday, while the Guardian said more than 11,000 flights were delayed or cancelled. More than 3,500 aircraft were in the air when the stop began. Eurocontrol suspended US-bound traffic from its member states at the FAA's request, and the Mirror reported that passengers at UK airports, including some Virgin Atlantic services from Manchester, faced knock-on disruption on Saturday. US officials expect normal service before Thanksgiving on 26 November.

## 7. A tanker strikes a docked river cruise ship on the Mississippi in New Orleans and capsizes it, with all passengers and crew accounted for and 32 people taken to hospital

A tanker has struck a river cruise ship docked on the Mississippi in New Orleans, leaving it on its side in the water, but all passengers and crew have been accounted for. The tanker Atlantic Asphalt hit American Cruise Lines' paddlewheel boat American Heritage at about 12:30pm local time on Friday, about two hours after it had docked to let passengers off. Thirty-two people were taken to hospital, none with critical injuries, and two harbour police officers were also treated.

New Orleans Mayor Helena Moreno said the tanker "lost control" and called the outcome "a miracle on the Mississippi", crediting first responders who "literally jumped right in to help". Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry used the same phrase, noting "the Mississippi River is not some quiet pond". The Coast Guard's Captain Melanie Burnham said there "seems to be some type of a loss of steering", and the exact cause is under investigation by the Coast Guard and the National Transportation Safety Board, whose officials were delayed by the national flight ground stop.

Accounts of how many people were aboard varied. The Port of New Orleans said about 129 passengers plus crew, its press secretary told the Guardian 197 passengers, and the mayor put the total at between 180 and 190. Footage showed people in life jackets being pulled from the river as the boat toppled over.

Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin plan to hold their first three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen next week, the White House has said.
