## 1. Badenoch vows to scrap inheritance tax on family homes

Kemi Badenoch used her closing speech to the Conservative conference in Birmingham on Wednesday to pledge that a Conservative government would scrap inheritance tax on family homes, whatever their value, and let couples pass on a further £1m in other assets tax-free. She said her ambition was to abolish the tax altogether "as soon as we can afford it," but that spending commitments on defence, prisons and the economy ruled that out for now. The party puts the cost at £6bn, to be paid for by cuts it says it has identified, including to the welfare bill, and says the number of families paying the tax (currently fewer than 5%) would fall by more than half. "This is my guarantee: no stamp duty when you buy your home; no mansion tax when you live in your home; and no inheritance tax on your home when you pass it to your children or grandchildren," she said.

Badenoch pitched the Conservatives as the "no-nonsense party" against what she called the Reform UK "circus," accused Prime Minister Andy Burnham of wanting to "live in the past," and attacked Reform politicians who suggest people of Asian and African heritage cannot be English, saying of Nigel Farage's party: "They are not like us. We are not like them." Labour and Reform have criticised the inheritance tax plan as help for a wealthy minority. Most of Thursday's national front pages lead on the speech.

## 2. Burnham meets Merz in Berlin to launch UK-Germany security pact

Prime Minister Andy Burnham travels to Berlin on Thursday for his first face-to-face meeting with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, where the two are launching a joint partnership to counter sabotage, cyber attacks and other hybrid threats, especially from Russia. The partnership will involve sharing information and co-ordinating action to monitor, deter and disrupt threats and to protect critical infrastructure. Downing Street said deeper co-operation against hybrid threats would be high on the agenda; last month Germany accused Russia of launching a drone fitted with explosives at Leipzig/Halle Airport. The leaders will also discuss support for Ukraine, the Middle East and disruption to trade routes such as the Strait of Hormuz, and convene a UK-Germany Regional Growth Forum with business leaders.

The visit follows Burnham's conference pledge to set out options for a closer relationship with the EU, including the prospect of eventually rejoining. ## 3. Reform clears its own officials over donations sting

Reform UK published a 27-page internal report concluding that two senior figures, Nigel Farage's aide Dan Jukes and the party's head of policy James Orr, did not break electoral law when they were filmed by undercover reporters discussing ways the party could receive foreign donations, including a proposed £500,000 gift. The footage, gathered by Verbatim Investigations, was broadcast by Channel 4 News in September. The report found the two had "said things that were wrong, careless and in one instance untrue," but concluded the £500,000 donation "did not take place" and that Reform had not commissioned polls paid for by the undercover reporters.

Both men stood down pending the inquiry and remain suspended while the Metropolitan Police continues a separate criminal investigation into possible offences under electoral law. The inquiry was carried out by the party's legal secretary, barrister Adam Richardson, and its general secretary, Tom Waterhouse. Reform said Verbatim had "acted unlawfully" and threatened legal action. Labour called the report a "whitewash," saying "you can find more whitewash in this report than at your local B&Q," and the Daily Mirror's front page reads "Reform not dodgy, says Reform."

## 4. UK consulate in East Jerusalem removes signs as Israel's closure deadline expires

Workers removed a plaque reading "British Consulate General" and a sign for the consular section from the UK mission in East Jerusalem overnight as Israel's deadline to shut it arrived on Thursday, Reuters reported; the union flag was still flying. Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar ordered the closure on 8 September in response to UK sanctions on Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, including a ban on importing goods made there, which Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband announced to the Commons. The United Nations and most countries regard the settlements as illegal under international law; Israel disputes this.

The consulate, first established in 1838, handles UK relations with Palestinians in Jerusalem, the West Bank and Gaza and is separate from the embassy in Tel Aviv. National Security Adviser Jonathan Powell made an undisclosed visit to Israel last week but left without an agreement. Israeli media and Reuters sources had reported that Britain was likely to expel Israeli diplomats (up to 27, according to Israeli media) if the closure went ahead; The Times now reports that Israel will strip the consulate of its diplomatic status but will not expel British diplomats, who can continue working from the Tel Aviv embassy.

## 5. UK borrowing costs hit 28-year high as Iran war squeezes public finances

## 6. Houthi attacks on Saudi airports kill three

## 10. Israeli strikes in Gaza continue on anniversary as ceasefire toll passes 1,400

Israel carried out new strikes in Gaza on Wednesday as it marked the third anniversary of the 7 October attacks, with Palestinian officials reporting five people killed, according to AFP. Al-Shifa hospital in Gaza City reported two people killed overnight; Gaza's civil defence identified one as Mohamed Tamous and said he was one of its volunteers. The Israeli military said it had killed "two Hamas terrorists who had exited Kamal Adwan Hospital," one of whom had planted explosives near the "Yellow Line" separating Israeli- and Hamas-held areas, and said Tamous was a Hamas sniper who also worked for the civil defence.
