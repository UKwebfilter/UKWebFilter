## 1. Millions of people are locked out of online benefits and pensions services after a cyber incident at the Department for Work and Pensions, as Downing Street refuses to rule out an attempted hack

Online services for pensions, universal credit and Pip applications were taken offline on Friday after what the Department for Work and Pensions called a "cyber incident", the BBC reported. The department said it had shut down its digital services as a "precaution" and that regular benefit and pension payments would still be made. Downing Street did not rule out an attempted hack but said there was "currently no evidence" that personal data had been lost, and an investigation is under way with the National Cyber Security Centre.

The BBC spoke to claimants who said money they rely on had not arrived. Samantha Reah, a mother of four in Cardiff, said she was missing £1,600 on her payday, while Georgina Howarth in Plymouth said she had to ask her mother for money for food. Claimants were also unable to update the online journal that shows they are looking for work; the DWP said nobody would be sanctioned for missed entries. The "Tell Us Once" service for reporting a death and the online Healthy Start application were also unavailable.

The UK's former chief technology officer David Knott told BBC Newsnight his "strong suspicion" was that it was an attack. The Guardian described "chaos" for claimants, experts quoted by the Daily Mail said a hostile state could be responsible, and the Independent reported calls for the government to "wake up" to the cyber threat. Reuters reported that the pensions minister called the incident "serious".

## 3. Schools in England will have to set aside time for pupils to read for pleasure every day in primary and every week in secondary, as the education secretary prepares to unveil a curriculum overhaul

Primary schools in England will have to set aside time every day for children to read for pleasure, and secondary pupils will get regular time each week to focus on entire books, poems and other literature, under changes to the national curriculum, the BBC and the Guardian reported. The education secretary, Lucy Powell, is expected to announce the plans in Parliament on Monday.

The Department for Education said reading enjoyment and daily reading levels "remain too low, particularly among disadvantaged children and boys", and that the change would help in the "battle for attention with mindless scrolling". Year 8 pupils will sit a mandatory reading check, and the government is setting a target to halve the reading disadvantage gap by 2035, with at least 80% of pupils meeting the expected standard, the BBC said. The Daily Mirror also led on the 2035 target.

"Across the world, we are seeing a ticking time bomb for our future generations, as reading and literacy levels are falling with too many children seeing reading as a chore or missing out altogether," Powell said. The Guardian reported that the wider overhaul could include shorter exams in some GCSE subjects, with history, geography and modern foreign languages the most likely to be slimmed down, and that secondary schools have been told not to start GCSE teaching early. Laura Trott, the shadow education secretary, welcomed the push but said it "must go hand in hand with a serious effort to reduce screen time in our classrooms".

## 5. Children in England who missed their school flu jab can get one at more than 6,000 pharmacies for the first time, as flu hospital admissions jump by almost 80% in a week

More than 6,000 pharmacies across England are offering flu vaccines to children who missed their jab at school, the first time the service has been available, Sky News reported, after a sharp rise in people admitted to hospital with flu. The UK Health Security Agency said hospital admission rates rose by almost 80% in a week, from 5.0 to 9.0 per 100,000 people, the highest at this point in November in any year this decade, according to the Daily Mirror.

The BBC reported that uptake is lagging. Only 34% of the almost 9.5 million people under 65 in clinical risk groups had been vaccinated by 22 November, leaving almost seven million without protection, and uptake among over-65s was just under 68%, lower than usual for the time of year. Suzanna McDonald, who leads the agency's flu programme, said the H1N1 strain was driving the spread and had "picked up a few more changes" than expected, though a drifted strain last year was still well matched by the vaccine.

Sir Jim Mackey, the outgoing chief executive of NHS England, said hospitals were approaching a "horrible spike in flu" and that some had reintroduced masks. The Guardian and the Sun reported urgent appeals for parents to get children vaccinated, and the BBC said the peak might not come until December or early January. In England last winter, an estimated 3,798 deaths were attributed to flu.

## 7. Hackers breach the US Air Force's flagship Cloud One computing platform, potentially exposing sensitive military and personnel data, the latest in a string of intrusions at US federal agencies

Hackers breached Cloud One, the US Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, in an attack that could have exposed sensitive military and personnel information, according to reports first published by Reuters and confirmed to NBC News by a US official. CBS News said the breach was discovered at some point in the last month during system upgrades. The Times of Israel reported that US Cyber Command had been called in to assess the damage.

An Air Force spokesperson declined to confirm or deny the breach, saying the service "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities, or operational status of specific networks and systems". The Defense Department later said its systems were "safe, secure, and managed 24-7 by the best experts and teams in the world", a message France 24 led on. Leidos, the main contractor running Cloud One since May, declined to comment specifically and referred questions to the Air Force, CBS reported.

The Independent said the system is tied to missile tracking and North American radar. The intrusion follows a series of breaches and outages reported at federal agencies this month, including the National Security Agency, the CIA, the FBI, the Department of Agriculture and the Federal Aviation Administration, whose problems led to a nationwide ground stop, according to CBS.
