## 1. Burnham calls almost a million young people out of work or training an economic catastrophe and moral failure

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has warned that the UK faces an "economic catastrophe and moral failure" because almost a million people aged 16 to 24 are not in education, employment or training, a group known as Neets. Writing in Monday's Times, he said the country should "invest in the success of our young people" rather than "pay for failure through the benefits system", adding that "benefits will become the backstop, not the starting point".

Burnham blamed two years of Covid lockdowns, social media that erodes young people's "confidence and sense of belonging", an education system focused on the "university route", too few job opportunities and the failures of previous governments. He said the welfare system currently offers "an assessment, a payment and, after that, goodbye", and that it would in future begin with young people's ambitions rather than their benefit entitlements, while the safety net would remain for those who cannot work.

The BBC reported that ministers are expected this week to announce tens of thousands of new youth apprenticeships and placements in construction and at accounting, consulting and legal firms. A full review of young people and work by former health secretary Alan Milburn is due next month. The Guardian and The Telegraph reported that Milburn wants money shifted from welfare into employment support, saying "this is not a workshy, snowflake generation". Shadow business secretary Julia Lopez said Labour had "been a disaster for youth jobs", blaming tax rises and red tape.

## 2. Children's commissioner says a postcode lottery is leaving services for children in a state of emergency

Services for children in England are in a "state of emergency" and young people face extreme and unfair inequality depending on where they live, according to a new report from the Children's Commissioner for England, Dame Rachel de Souza. Her report, called the Childhood Lottery, analysed a hundred measures covering health and social care, education, safety and play, and concluded that a "postcode lottery" has produced a "litany of failures", the BBC reported.

The report found that deprivation alone could not explain the differences. Haringey in north London scored highly despite high deprivation, while Cambridge ranked lower despite being one of the least deprived areas. Middlesbrough, Hounslow, Bradford, Nottingham, Halton, Peterborough, Leicester, Liverpool, Sunderland and Medway were ranked the hardest places to grow up, while Cheshire East, Merton, Surrey and Richmond upon Thames were among the least challenging.

The gaps were stark, the BBC said. A child waited six days for a mental health referral in Leicester but 118 days in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight. In Richmond upon Thames, 70% of children got good English and maths GCSEs, against 31% in Knowsley, and four council areas spent nothing on youth services per teenager. The Metro, which led on the report, said children in Blackpool were 27 times more likely to miss school than those in Lambeth. Dame Rachel called for minimum standards for children's services, an emergency fund for improvements and a bigger role for large councils and mayors.

A firearm was found at the scene and could be seen within the police cordon, the force said. The force said there would be a heightened police presence while the investigation continued.

Assistant Chief Constable Claire Rukas called it "a shocking incident and a sobering reminder of the potential dangers faced by officers", and appealed for anyone with information to come forward, saying "even the smallest bit of information could make all the difference". Nottinghamshire Police and Crime Commissioner Gary Godden thanked members of the public who helped the injured officer and said firearms incidents like this were "extremely rare". Nottingham City Council leader Neghat Khan said she was "deeply shocked and saddened" and that every available resource was being deployed. ## 4. Passengers who play loud music on buses could face £100 fines in antisocial behaviour crackdown

Passengers who play loud music on buses in England could be fined up to £100 under a government crackdown on antisocial behaviour on public transport, the BBC reported. The measures also target littering, vaping and threatening behaviour. The government said it had issued new guidance to local authorities on powers introduced earlier this year, which let them set new rules on buses, subject to sign-off by the transport secretary.

The government said bus drivers would not be responsible for enforcement, which would fall to authorised officers including Transport Safety Officers and the police. Under existing powers on the railways, passengers who refuse to stop playing music can be removed from a train or fined up to £1,000, a figure the Mirror and the Express put in their headlines. A national campaign asking rail passengers to "share the carriage, not the noise" is also being launched across operator websites, social media and carriages.

"We've all been on the train when someone starts blasting music or videos from their phone. It gets on your nerves and can quickly spoil the journey," said Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander said small changes in behaviour could make a big difference. The TSSA union called the move "a step in the right direction" but urged ministers to confront violence and abuse against passengers and staff and to fund British Transport Police. The Campaign for Better Transport welcomed it but questioned how the rules would be enforced.

## 5. Up to 20 maternity units in England may have to shut, NHS leader warns

Up to 20 maternity units in England may have to close or change their role to ensure safe care for mothers and babies, an NHS leader has warned. Matthew Hopkins, director for acute care at the NHS Alliance, told the Guardian that centralising childbirth services in fewer hospitals was "inevitable" and "the right thing", and urged ministers and MPs to show "courage" and "grasp the nettle", even though it would mean some expectant mothers travelling further.

Hopkins said that as many as 20 of England's 155 consultant-led units would need to shut or change, and that smaller units delivering 1,500 to 2,000 babies a year were becoming increasingly unsustainable because of a shortage of obstetricians and a sharp rise in caesarean sections. Many of the units most at risk are in rural or coastal areas, he said, adding that the current configuration was "not fit for purpose". The BBC reported the same warning under the headline that maternity care organisation is not fit for purpose.

Hospital bosses fear the government's maternity taskforce, due to report before Christmas, will be "tokenistic" if it does not address the rise in caesareans, the Guardian said. The comments provoked an immediate outcry. Hannah Leonard of the Royal College of Midwives said women and families "rely on local maternity units to be there when they need them". Dr Alison Wright, president of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, said there were "other solutions to our workforce challenges that are far safer than closing maternity units".

## 7. Trump defends easing Russia sanctions and says Zelensky never wanted to end the war

US President Donald Trump has defended his decision to suspend a range of sanctions on Russia, saying he did not want to "do anything that will drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table" while he negotiates peace in Ukraine.
