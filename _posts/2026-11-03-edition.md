## 1. Shabana Mahmood drops plans to make care workers wait up to 15 years to settle in the UK, but most migrants will still face a 10-year wait

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has scrapped plans to make foreign care workers already in the UK wait up to 15 years to qualify for permanent settlement, the BBC reported. Under the revised policy, care workers who earn at least £25,000 a year and arrived before May 2025 will continue to qualify for indefinite leave to remain after five years. Mahmood had proposed doubling the standard wait from five years to 10 for most migrants.

The Guardian reported that the climbdown followed a cabinet backlash and pressure from health unions, but that most of the new rules remain. The wait for those earning under £50,000 will double to 10 years, and people who arrived by irregular means such as small boats could face up to 30 years. Mahmood told the Commons that settlement "must be earned".

Asked by the BBC whether she had made a U-turn, Mahmood said "I don't accept that", adding that some 500,000 people "will not now settle at five years". She declined to say what the change would cost. The Conservatives said it would cost taxpayers billions of pounds, Reform UK leader Nigel Farage called it "the beginning of the capitulation", and some Labour MPs and the Law Society criticised the retrospective nature of the remaining changes.

## 3. AI-powered malware is 'highly likely' to be used to attack UK power stations, airports and hospitals by 2028, the National Cyber Security Centre warns

AI-powered malicious software is "highly likely" to be used in hacking attacks against the UK's power stations, airports and hospitals by 2028, the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) has said, The Guardian reported. The warning came in the annual review of the NCSC, which is part of GCHQ, and said AI would probably enable a ransomware attack on critical national infrastructure, which covers energy, transport, defence, food, health and finance.

The review also warned that Russia and China will start using AI "routinely" in cyber-attacks. The NCSC said AI would boost the ransomware threat by helping attackers scout targets, find weaknesses in IT networks and write malicious code. Past UK ransomware victims include Royal Mail, Capita and several councils.

NCSC chief executive Richard Horne was due to warn in a speech on Tuesday that "in the coming months, we face a real prospect of an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents", blaming weaknesses that have been "tolerated for far too long". Sky News led its coverage with a warning to businesses to prepare for cyber attacks or face empty shelves, and The Times reported the warning of an "unprecedented rise" in AI attacks. Last month the government urged households to keep bottled water and three days' worth of food in case of a weather, cyber or other serious emergency.

## 4. Supermarket fridges and freezers in seven European countries are hit by a new suspected cyber-attack as UK officials say they are monitoring the threat

Dozens of supermarkets across seven European countries have been hit by a cyber-attack affecting their refrigerators and freezers, the BBC reported. Around 40 Delhaize stores in Belgium and Luxembourg were affected, along with smaller numbers in Sweden, Ireland, the Netherlands, Estonia and Finland. Freezer temperatures reportedly rose as high as 50C.

Sabcobel, a Belgian company that makes some of the affected units, said their evaporators were switched to defrost mode, the same method believed to have been used last month when hundreds of French supermarkets were hit. Its chief executive said the units needed physical changes, not just software updates. It is unclear who is behind the latest attack.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre said it was working with international partners to understand the incident, and no incidents have been reported in the UK, the BBC said. The Guardian reported that EU food and agriculture commissioner Christophe Hansen called the attacks "a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain". One Delhaize branch said it had lost €100,000 of produce. In the US, authorities are investigating refrigeration failures at military bases in August and working with French officials on whether the incidents are linked. The Times reported that British meat suppliers had been hit.

## 5. British troops in any future Ukraine reassurance force would be allowed to fire back if attacked by Russia, as Britain and France prepare a 2,800-strong rehearsal in Poland

Russia has opposed foreign troops in Ukraine, and President Vladimir Putin has said they would be "legitimate targets". Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said: "This is not a hypothetical plan, it's a real force, ready to help Ukraine once the fighting stops." ## 6. The US carries out what officials call limited, defensive strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz on the eve of the midterm elections

The US has launched strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, US officials told CBS, the BBC reported. Officials described the strikes as limited and said they were a response to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps firing on a US aircraft. Iranian state news agency IRNA reported several explosions on the island on Monday evening, and Al Jazeera said blasts were also heard in the port city of Bandar Lengeh.

NBC News reported that two US officials said the attacks were not a resumption of major combat operations, and a third called them "a defensive measure for Iranians using surface-to-air missiles against US assets". The Guardian reported that the Wall Street Journal described the attacks as part of a series of smaller strikes in recent weeks that had not been made public. Earlier on Monday the IRGC said it had captured two US drones and destroyed an MQ-9.

The strikes came hours after Vice-President JD Vance said he and President Donald Trump felt "pretty good" about the prospects of a negotiated settlement, and on the eve of midterm elections in which the unpopular war has been a vulnerability for Republicans.
