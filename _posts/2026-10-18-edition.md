## 1. SAS soldiers accused of murder were sent back to war zones, Sunday Times investigation says

The Sunday Times leads with an investigation reporting that Special Air Service soldiers accused of murder were sent back into war zones while they were still under police investigation. According to the paper, as summarised in the BBC's review of the Sunday front pages, five men were arrested on secret bases in Syria and Iraq in 2022, starting what it calls an "unprecedented three-year criminal investigation" that "plunged the regiment into crisis".

The Times reports that the five were returned to the war zone just 48 hours after being questioned by police, and were then "left in limbo for three years" before being told that no charges would be brought. All of the former suspects have since left the military, the paper says, and they have written to former Prime Minister Sir Keir Starmer to complain about what they describe as their "potentially unlawful arrests". The paper's Insight team has published the findings alongside a podcast, a video and a guide to its key revelations.

A Ministry of Defence spokesperson said personnel are "entitled to expect that any investigation is conducted fairly and professionally", adding that there are procedures in place for people to report concerns. "If allegations of wrongdoing arise, it is right that they are properly assessed and, where appropriate, investigated," the spokesperson said.

## 3. Fifteen NHS staff now sacked for looking at Nottingham attack victims' records

Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust has now sacked 15 members of staff for inappropriately accessing the medical records of the victims of the 2023 Nottingham attacks, up from 11 confirmed in May, the BBC and Sky News reported. Students Barnaby Webber and Grace O'Malley-Kumar and grandfather Ian Coates were stabbed to death on 13 June 2023 by Valdo Calocane, who had been diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia in 2020 and is serving an indefinite hospital order in a high-security facility.

The trust said 15 final written warnings and two first written warnings had also been issued, two cases had no case to answer, and action had been taken against doctors, nurses, other registered healthcare professionals and other staff. Regulators including the Nursing and Midwifery Council, the General Medical Council and the Information Commissioner's Office, as well as Nottinghamshire Police, have been informed. Investigations are continuing, including into access to the records of the surviving victims.

Grace's father, Dr Sanjoy Kumar, told the BBC he was "appalled", saying the most confidential data was being looked at by "voyeuristic people in the NHS". The trust's medical director, Dr Manjeet Shehmar, said there was "simply no excuse" for accessing a patient's records without a legitimate reason and apologised to the families. She said a new system to detect possible data breaches is being introduced, alongside a campaign to educate staff about data protection.

## 4. BBC rejects Sunday paper claims over civil service fact-checkers and Bitesize

The BBC is facing questions over its impartiality from two Sunday papers, and has rejected both sets of claims. The Sunday Telegraph reported that civil service fact-checkers have been embedded in the BBC, a revelation it says has raised questions about the broadcaster's impartiality, according to the BBC's own review of the Sunday front pages. A BBC spokesperson denied that civil servants had any editorial influence on stories, saying all editorial decision-making was carried out by BBC journalists and editors.

The Mail on Sunday's main story accused the BBC of "brainwashing children", alleging that its education website, BBC Bitesize, is pushing "'extreme' Left-Wing views" on millions of pupils. The paper reported that the site included an online game called "Increasing Immigration", which it said rewarded students for choosing to open borders and grant an amnesty to "illegal migrants".

A BBC spokesman said Bitesize works with educational consultants to develop resources that help young people explore and understand a wide range of topics, in line with the curriculums across the UK. "While we don't accept the characterisation of Bitesize in this story, we regularly review content to ensure it remains relevant and to ensure it meets the BBC's editorial standards and will review these examples in that context," the spokesman said. The two stories are separate, but both question whether the BBC keeps outside influence and political views out of its output.

## 5. Health secretary warns of tougher NHS winter as £140m goes to urgent care

The NHS faces a more challenging winter than normal after a summer of record heat, Health Secretary Yvette Cooper has warned, as she announced a £140m increase in funding for urgent care in England. Cooper said the health service had been preparing since July for the impact of seasonal illnesses. "Winter will be more challenging for our NHS this year, after such a heavy summer," she said, according to the Guardian. "That's why we have been preparing early and investing in extra urgent care capacity to help get patients the care they need."

The money will go to 14 schemes across the country, including new urgent care centres, ambulance fleets and upgrades to emergency departments. The Guardian reported that almost 7.3 million people attended A&E between June and August, the highest figure for a summer period, and that the NHS national medical director, Prof Frankie Swords, warned earlier this month of "ominous" signs of an early flu season.

Emma Rowland of NHS England said the winter "will be tough" and urged eligible people to get vaccinated against flu and other viruses. Patricia Marquis of the Royal College of Nursing England welcomed the money in the short term but called it "just another sticking plaster on a system that is at breaking point", urging ministers to use the Budget, now weeks away, to invest in the nursing workforce. Sky News, The Telegraph and the Independent all led on Cooper's warning that the winter will be "more challenging" than normal.

## 6. More than 1,200 arrested as protests across France turn violent

More than 1,200 people were arrested across France on Saturday as a day of protests against hardship in schools and the cost of living turned violent in several cities, with buildings set alight and shops looted. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said 150 police officers were injured. The BBC reported an estimated 550,000 people took to the streets; TASS quoted a trade union putting the figure at more than 500,000, while France 24 reported organisers claiming 800,000.

In Limoges, the 143-year-old town hall was "largely lost" to a fire set by masked protesters, the regional prefect told AFP, as quoted by the Guardian, calling the damage a "catastrophe for heritage". Lyon's historic tourist office was burned, protesters briefly broke into Marseille's town hall, and in Paris police used tear gas and water cannon around Place de la République as shops along Boulevard Magenta were looted. In Saint-Étienne, the mayor said a young man "had his hand blown off by a grenade".

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez blamed "opportunistic delinquents" for hijacking the demonstrations but said no state of emergency was planned. The protests began last month at schools in the poor northern suburbs of Paris over teacher absences and shabby facilities, and on Saturday were joined by trade unions and the revived gilets jaunes (yellow vests) movement. Fox News reported that the outgoing President Emmanuel Macron vowed "prosecution and punishment" after a historic building burned.

## 7. Masked settlers attack European diplomats and a French mayor in the West Bank

Masked Israeli settlers attacked a delegation of European diplomats in the occupied West Bank on Saturday, Belgium said, in the latest violence linked to the olive harvest. The diplomats, from Belgium, France, Poland and the Catholic Church, had gone to the largely Christian village of Taybeh near Ramallah to assess the situation after near-daily settler attacks on Palestinians harvesting olives, the BBC reported. A Belgian consulate car had a window smashed and a French mayor was assaulted. The Guardian named him as Bernard Baudoux, mayor of Aulnoye-Aymeries, who was knocked to the ground while wearing his sash of office, and reported that three people were injured and the archbishop of Cambrai was also attacked.

Belgium's foreign minister, Maxime Prévot, said "impunity for violent settlers must end" and that Belgium would keep pressing for EU sanctions. The Israel Defense Forces condemned "the severe act of violence" by "Israeli rioters" and said they were investigating, and Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar also condemned it while saying the visit had not been coordinated with the army in advance. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office cast doubt on what happened and suggested it was part of a "coordinated campaign to smear Israel".

The attack came a day after Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine and several Palestinians were injured by settlers in another village. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio called that attack "disturbing", and Al Jazeera reported that he wants a "crackdown" on settler violence.
