## 1. Army chief tells Britain to prepare for war as troops rehearse Ukraine mission

The head of the British Army, General Sir Roly Walker, has told soldiers that Britain "is going to need you to fight", The Times reported, in an address the Daily Express called "extraordinary". According to the BBC's review of the papers, he warned that political leaders will call on "ordinary Britons" to prepare for war. Sky News reported him saying politicians will start a "frank conversation" with the public about getting ready for conflict, while The Telegraph, the Independent and Metro all led on his warning that Britons will have to make "sacrifices".

His remarks came as Defence Secretary Wes Streeting announced, on his first visit to the US in the role, that UK forces will take part in a "major exercise" in Poland next month alongside French and German troops. The Times, which called the drill the first of its kind, reported that at least 1,500 troops from the three countries will join Polish forces to simulate moving soldiers and supplies such as food and fuel into Ukraine in the event of a ceasefire. The exercise was agreed at a coalition of the willing summit in July. Streeting said it would "ensure we are ready to support the military training and post-war rebuilding that Ukraine will need". The Mirror noted that the announcement came as President Trump declined to say directly whether Vladimir Putin started the war, telling reporters: "I want that war ended."

## 2. Police assess Palestine Action post by wife of national security adviser

The Metropolitan Police is assessing a social media post by Sarah Helm, the wife of the government's national security adviser Jonathan Powell, which referred to Palestine Action, a group proscribed as a terrorist organisation. Helm, an author and former Sunday Times and Independent journalist, wrote on X: "God help us authors who support Palestine and Palestine Action." She was responding to a Sky News discussion about the novelist Sally Rooney, whose books will no longer be supplied to UK booksellers after she said she would use her royalties to support the group.

The Met said the post was "in the process of being assessed to determine whether any further police investigation may be required". A No 10 spokeswoman said Helm was "an individual who does not represent the government in any capacity". Reform UK MP Danny Kruger wrote to the prime minister, and the Campaign Against Antisemitism called the post "gobsmacking", saying Powell must publicly denounce it if he is to stay in post. Green Party leader Zack Polanski said Helm was "speaking absolute common sense". The Guardian reported that, under government vetting guidance, marriage to someone tied to a proscribed group is normally treated as a security risk. Since the ban last year, about 3,700 people have been arrested for showing support for the group, and a legal challenge to the proscription is due at the Supreme Court next month, the BBC said.

## 3. Healey backs case for Heathrow third runway as Burnham holds back

Chancellor John Healey has said the case for a third runway at Heathrow "has always been a case for growth", striking a more positive note than Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who has declined to say whether he backs the expansion. Speaking at the International Monetary Fund meetings in Bangkok, Healey said airports across the UK had been "a big part of jobs and growth" in recent decades, but that the government would first study responses to a public consultation on the plans, which has now closed.

Burnham has said the decision is "a matter for London and Londoners", and the Guardian cited a Sunday Times report that he told Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander he was "unconvinced" by the arguments. Healey's predecessor, Rachel Reeves, had made the runway a centrepiece of her growth agenda. Heathrow's chief executive, Thomas Woldbye, warned this week that not building it would be a "huge missed opportunity", and the BBC reported that the airport's owners want explicit backing from the prime minister before the end of the year. The £33bn project would raise the cap on flights from 480,000 to 720,000 a year and require the M25 to be rerouted through a tunnel, the BBC said. Opening has slipped from 2035 to a suggested 2039. Healey voted for the runway in 2018 and is preparing his 28 October Budget, the Guardian noted.

## 4. RAF jets reported near-misses with mystery drones over the North Sea

RAF jets had two close encounters with unidentified drones over the North Sea earlier this year, The Telegraph reported on its front page, citing incident logs it had seen. The incidents happened just days apart and were reported to police, and several external investigations are under way, the paper said.

According to the reports, the first encounter came on 18 February, when an RAF Voyager tanker was refuelling a Typhoon fighter off Cromer in Norfolk and an object was seen at 17,000ft, more than 40 times the legal height limit for commercial drones. It passed so close that the tanker's pilot instinctively ducked. On 23 February, a Typhoon practising dogfights about 30 miles off Humberside saw a black circular object, about the size of a car tyre, pass around 300ft overhead. Neither drone showed up on radar. The Mirror noted there was no evidence linking the sightings to hostile powers, but that the altitude was beyond most commercially available craft, and any private operator would need military permission to enter the training areas. It also cited an International Institute for Strategic Studies report that the Kremlin had been accused of a drone surveillance campaign over military bases in the UK and Europe between August 2024 and February 2026, likely launched from its shadow fleet of tankers. The incidents came days before the start of the US war with Iran.

## 6. Trump puts new Russia sanctions on hold days before they take effect

President Donald Trump has signed an executive order waiving sanctions on Russia that Congress passed and he signed into law in September, two days before they were due to take effect. In the order, Trump said he had certified to Congress that the waiver "is in the national interests of the United States", and DW reported that it will remain in place until he or his officials decide "further action is appropriate". The White House did not immediately explain how the pause serves the national interest.

The law, named after the late Republican Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July, would have allowed tariffs of up to 100% on the five biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas, most significantly China and India, and sanctions on Vladimir Putin, senior officials, banks and Russia's shadow fleet of tankers, the BBC said. A bipartisan group of 20 lawmakers had urged Trump on Thursday to apply it in full. The move follows Trump's deal last week to buy Russian diesel to lower US fuel prices before November's midterm elections, which drew criticism from both parties. Democratic Congressman Don Beyer called it "Trump's latest surrender to Putin". Kirill Dmitriev, Vladimir Putin's special representative for investment and economic cooperation, called it "an important and truly historic moment", TASS reported. The Independent's front page said the White House was also looking at easing sanctions on the shadow fleet as Ukraine runs low on Patriot missiles.
