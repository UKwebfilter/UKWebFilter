## 1. Andy Burnham tells MPs it would be 'unthinkable' to take the UK back into the EU without a referendum, as critics say he has opened the door to a second Brexit vote

Andy Burnham has said it would be "unthinkable" for the UK to rejoin the European Union without another referendum, his clearest commitment yet to putting any return to a public vote. Making a Commons statement on Friday's Brussels summit, the prime minister was asked by the Conservative MP Sir Bernard Jenkin for an unequivocal statement that no prime minister could take Britain back in without one. "Yes, it would be unthinkable," he replied, although he said he was not proposing a second referendum "at this moment".

Burnham told MPs he would honour Labour's manifesto red lines on Europe in this Parliament, but that work he had set in train would inform decisions "in the Parliament beyond this one". He said there was "no fixed opinion" and "no fixed destination" in No 10, and that a plan setting out options for closer ties would be published next month. The Guardian reported that Downing Street insiders expect him, if he concludes rejoining is best, to seek a negotiating mandate in Labour's next manifesto and then hold a confirmatory vote.

He dismissed warnings from former prime ministers including Theresa May, Rishi Sunak and David Cameron, saying they were "stuck in the past". Kemi Badenoch accused him of betraying a promise made in the Makerfield by-election not to reopen the Brexit debate, and the Labour MP Graham Stringer warned of a loss of trust. Legislation to implement the food and drink part of last week's deal is due next week.

## 2. The Timms review of Personal Independence Payment is published today and is expected to recommend fast-tracked awards and fewer reassessments for the most severely disabled, but no major savings

A government-commissioned review of Personal Independence Payment (Pip), the main disability benefit, is due to be published on Tuesday. The BBC reported it is set to recommend that people with the most severe conditions and terminal illnesses get quicker payments, and that assessments be overhauled to give claimants in England and Wales more appropriate support. It is understood not to propose major savings.

The review is chaired by Disability Minister Sir Stephen Timms and was set up last year after the Starmer government's abandoned attempt to cut welfare spending. Writing in The Times, Sir Stephen said those with the most severe and persistent conditions, believed to be about one in 10 claimants, would be fast-tracked with no further assessments. He told MPs on Monday the review would call for "a fundamental overhaul to rehumanise Pip", that it should remain a "cash-first" benefit, and that claimants might choose to give up part of their award for services, along the lines of the Motability scheme.

About four million people claim Pip. The government expects to spend £32.1bn on it this year, rising to £41.5bn in 2030-31, compared with £16.3bn in 2019-20 at today's prices. Disability groups, which helped draft the report, have broadly welcomed it, and the BBC said concerns about means-testing or vouchers are not borne out. The Adam Smith Institute called for tighter eligibility for lower-level mental health claims, and the Conservatives said Labour was "not serious" about fixing welfare. The government will respond to the proposals.

## 3. Victims of crime are being failed 'at every step' of the justice system, the victims' commissioner warns, as nearly half of crown court cases in her survey were postponed

Victims of crime in England and Wales are being failed at every stage of the criminal justice system, according to the annual survey by the Victims' Commissioner, Claire Waxman, published on Tuesday. The report, based on more than 6,000 responses, found that 28% of victims chose not to report crimes to the police, rising to 39% among victims of rape and sexual assault. Nearly half (47%) of those whose cases went to the crown court saw their trial postponed, and in 28% of cases the trial took place more than a year after first scheduled.

The survey found 46% were dissatisfied with police communication, and more than half had to phone officers for updates. Only 58% received support within a month of referral, and about one in seven knew their rights under the Victims' Code. Waxman said victims were "still being let down by a criminal justice system that too often treats them as an afterthought", adding: "After more than a decade of decline, the cracks in the system are too deep to paper over."

She told the BBC the state was "failing" to keep the public safe, criticised letters sent to victims ahead of early prisoner releases as "very poor", and urged Andy Burnham to put justice reform in his 10-year plan. She is recommending a victim identifier number to track cases and an "end-to-end" review. Victims Minister Alex Davies-Jones said the government was doing "everything we can to fix the broken system we inherited" and would update the Victims' Code next year.

## 4. One in eight vapes seized from pupils in a Metropolitan Police pilot in south-west London schools were found to contain drugs including spice and THC

Vapes confiscated from schoolchildren in south-west London have been found to contain illicit drugs, in what the Metropolitan Police described as the first investigation of its kind. At least four of the 32 devices tested in the pilot, one in eight, contained substances such as cannabinoids and the synthetic drug spice, according to the force, and a further three are awaiting laboratory results but are believed to contain illicit materials.

The pilot was carried out amid growing concern over young people collapsing and being taken to hospital after inhaling substances hidden in vaping products. Officers used specialist kits designed to detect whether vapes had been spiked, technology that has more recently also been deployed in London's night-time economy to tackle drink and drug spiking. Deputy Assistant Commissioner Ben Russell said the results showed "evidence of an emerging threat to children", adding: "Whether knowingly or unknowingly, children carrying dangerous substances in schools is concerning."

The Met said it had received around 200 reports over the past year linked to spiking offences involving child victims, with many young people becoming unexpectedly ill after using vapes they believed to be safe. The Daily Mail noted an earlier University of Bath study that found up to a quarter of vapes confiscated from secondary schools in England contained spice. In April, a 17-year-old student at a college in Kingston lost consciousness after allegedly using a vape believed to be laced with drugs, and forensic analysis later found two products contained a Class B drug. Police are urging anyone who thinks they have been spiked to report it quickly, as evidence can be lost within 12 to 24 hours.

## 5. The UK sanctions five senior Myanmar military commanders after the UN's top court ruled that the country committed genocide against the Rohingya

The UK has imposed sanctions on five senior Myanmar military leaders, days after the International Court of Justice ruled that the country carried out genocide against its Rohingya minority, the first time a state has been found guilty of the crime. The commanders named are Myo Htut Hlaing, Myo Min Tun, Myat Thet Oo, Kyaw Soe and Kyaw Swar Oo. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband told MPs that Myanmar's president, Min Aung Hlaing, already sanctioned by the UK in 2021, was "directly responsible for many of the atrocities" as head of the military in 2017.

"Our response to a crime as profoundly shocking as genocide must reflect our values and must be matched by clear action," Miliband said. He confirmed new legislation to stop the supply of aviation fuel to Myanmar, called for a pause in hostilities, an end to air strikes on civilians and the release of political prisoners, and said the UK would raise the issue at the UN Security Council this week. Last week he expelled Myanmar's defence attaché after the ruling.

The court found that "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference" from the evidence, by nine votes to six, and determined that about 10,000 Rohingya were murdered during the 2017 clearance operation in Rakhine state. Myanmar denied the claims. The UK was joined by countries including Australia, Belgium, Portugal, Spain and Canada in acknowledging the ruling. In the Guardian, former prime minister Gordon Brown argued that words were not enough and set out further steps.

## 6. Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang as chief executive after Donald Trump says he 'convinced' the board, adding about $1tn to its value and drawing bipartisan anger in Congress

ABC News reported that Trump owned at least $12m of Nvidia stock at the end of 2025; the White House says his portfolio is managed independently.

Democrats including Hakeem Jeffries and Elizabeth Warren called the decision indefensible and reckless, and Republicans including Tom Cotton, Don Bacon and Pete Ricketts demanded answers. On the same day the Commerce Department moved to blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore firm Alice Huang ran. Huang is due to answer questions from House committees on 1 December.

## 15. Denmark's Supreme Court upholds the country's 'ghetto law', backing evictions designed to reduce the share of 'non-Western' residents on social housing estates

Denmark's Supreme Court has upheld the country's 2018 "ghetto law", ruling in favour of a housing association that terminated 91 leases to stop its estate being labelled a "hard ghetto". DW reported that the law allows the relocation of "non-Western" residents from minority-heavy neighbourhoods in the name of integration, and requires social housing estates to rebuild, renovate and adjust their social mix if they become minority-heavy.

The ruling upheld a 2022 lower court decision against seven tenants in Nojsomhed, Helsingor, north of Copenhagen, who had fought their evictions. The court found the law was not discriminatory in its treatment of immigrants and their descendants from non-Western countries, diverging from a decision by the EU's top court last year that found its application could amount to discrimination. Housing Minister Jacob Mark welcomed the decision, saying it "strengthens security, social cohesion and integration". Denmark's tough immigration policies have made it a model for some EU governments, and DW noted it was among five countries that last week announced plans for an asylum deportation hub in Rwanda.
