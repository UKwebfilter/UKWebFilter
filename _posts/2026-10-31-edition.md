## 1. Andy Burnham says he is open to a formal apology for Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade, but rules out reparations

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is "open" to the UK issuing a formal apology for its role in the transatlantic slave trade, but has ruled out financial reparations, saying they were "not the right answer", the BBC reported. Writing in The Guardian ahead of a Commonwealth leaders' summit in the Caribbean next week, he offered a "structured dialogue" with those affected and said listening to calls for a formal apology on behalf of the British state would form "an important part of this process".

The Guardian said he went further than any previous prime minister, sharing his "sorrow and regret" for the suffering of millions. He wrote that he did not think British people today can be "held liable for historical wrongs" in which they played no personal part. The British government has never formally apologised for slavery.

Barbados Prime Minister Mia Mottley welcomed the statement while still seeking reparatory justice, the BBC said, but Denis O'Brien of The Repair Campaign called it disappointing. The comments come as King Charles tours the Caribbean, where The Guardian said protesters calling for "reparations now" are expected at the Antigua summit.

## 5. Shabana Mahmood is expected to soften plans for a longer wait for permanent settlement, with social care workers likely to be exempt

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is expected to announce her final decision on rules for permanent settlement next week, with social care workers already in the UK now likely to be exempt from a longer wait, the BBC reported. She had proposed extending the qualifying period for indefinite leave to remain from five years to 10, with some lower-paid migrants facing a 15-year wait.

The BBC said most other workers are still likely to have to live and work in the UK for 10 years before becoming eligible, although that could be reduced for those who contribute to their communities or earn higher incomes. The consultation drew 200,000 responses, and the Home Office will not comment until the decisions are presented to Parliament.

Mahmood has faced opposition within government. Before rejoining the cabinet, Housing Secretary Angela Rayner described the proposals as "un-British" because they changed the rules for people who had come in good faith. The Guardian reported that fellow ministers were angered by her failure to reveal the impact of the shake-up, and The Sun described a climbdown amid fears of a Labour revolt. A Conservative spokesperson had earlier accused Mahmood of caving in to pressure.

## 7. Riot police are deployed at Poland's constitutional court as President Nawrocki forces his way in after its chief judge is suspended

The court said those involved were "committing the crime of a coup d'etat", while Tusk wrote that "restoring the rule of law is our duty".
