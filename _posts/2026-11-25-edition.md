## 1. The Timms review declares Personal Independence Payment 'no longer fit for purpose', backing fast-track awards for the most severely disabled and a new extra-costs test, but setting out no firm savings

The long-awaited review of Personal Independence Payment, led by the disability minister Sir Stephen Timms, has concluded that the main disability benefit in England and Wales is "no longer fit for purpose and requires a fundamental reset". Timms told MPs on Tuesday that support "should correspond more closely to genuine additional costs and needs".

Two recommendations have been accepted immediately by the government: a faster route to an award for people with severe conditions, and fewer repeat reviews for those whose conditions are unlikely to improve. For the remaining claimants, the 140-page report proposes assessments tailored to individual circumstances, replacing today's four payment bands of £30.30 to £194.60 a week. It says the benefit "is not appropriate for those who do not incur any additional costs associated with being disabled", although assessments would not ask for receipts. It also calls for an independent advocacy service and an end to pausing payments during long hospital stays.

Co-author Sharon Brennan said some people "might get less". The Resolution Foundation estimates that about 666,000 claimants say they have no extra disability costs, and that ending their payments could save up to £4.4bn a year by 2029-30. Officials say no decision has been taken on whether changes would apply to existing claimants. The Institute for Fiscal Studies warned that easier claims would likely push spending up, and the Conservatives called the review "a complete farce". Ministers must now weigh it against last week's Milburn report in a welfare White Paper due in the new year.

## 2. An independent review finds the Metropolitan Police is still institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic three years after the Casey report, and warns further scandals are 'highly likely'

Britain's biggest police force remains institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic, according to a review of the Metropolitan Police's progress since Baroness Casey's 2023 report, which was commissioned after the murder of Sarah Everard by a serving officer. The 389-page review by Dr Gillian Fairfield, a public health leader commissioned by the Mayor of London, Sadiq Khan, concluded: "We judge these fundamental, systemic issues remain."

The review found the Met had not made good progress on any of Casey's 14 recommendations, with some progress in nine areas and limited progress in five. It described "institutional defensiveness" and said "the only scrutiny the Met really fears is from the media". One female officer told the team there was "real predatory behaviour" towards younger women and new recruits. Bereaved families accused the force of victim-blaming and gaslighting, and the report detailed forensic exhibits, statements and CCTV going missing in one homicide investigation. It warned further scandals were "highly likely" without change, and made ten new recommendations.

"The Met struggles to acknowledge racism. But if it can't name it, it can't face it," Fairfield told journalists. The commissioner, Sir Mark Rowley, accepted the recommendations but said he would not be drawn into "a politicised debate about certain labels", adding that 1,800 officers and staff had been removed and that London was safer. Khan urged him to respond "with humility and reflection, not hubris" and to provide Londoners with "the police service they deserve".

## 4. One in three 16 to 24-year-olds in Britain have never had sex, up from one in five in the previous survey, as a major national sex survey finds adults are having less sex but more contact online

A third of 16 to 24-year-olds in Britain say they have never had sex, up from about one in five between 1990 and 2010, according to the fourth National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal), published across five papers in the Lancet. The survey of nearly 7,000 adults, carried out in 2022-24, found that 38% of young women and 28% of young men had never had intercourse.

Sex is becoming less frequent across the board. Adults under 45 now have sex about twice a month on average, compared with four times a month in 1990-91 and three times in 2010-12. For the first time, the survey asked about online behaviour: half of 16 to 24-year-olds had sent or received sexual images in the past year, and 86% of young men and about half of young women said they had watched pornography. The researchers noted that pornography use was less common among people who had never had sex, suggesting online activity is not simply replacing in-person sex, though they said they could not be certain. The survey also recorded a substantial drop in the share of young women who want children.

The study does not establish causes. Researchers are examining factors including less face-to-face socialising and drinking, young people living with their parents for longer, poorer mental health and the rise of digital technology. Soazig Clifton of University College London, one of the Natsal team, said it was "relatively unusual to see big differences like this".

## 5. Andy Burnham promises nuclear test veterans 'the full truth' as he sets up a Hillsborough-style independent panel into blood tests and the health legacy of Britain's Cold War weapons trials

Andy Burnham has told veterans of Britain's nuclear weapons tests that they will get "the full truth", confirming an independent panel modelled on the Hillsborough inquiry to examine the health legacy of the programme. The panel will be chaired by James Jones, the former Bishop of Liverpool who chaired the Hillsborough Independent Panel, and will aim to publish an interim report in time for the 75th anniversary of the first UK nuclear weapons test.

Between 1952 and 1967, about 22,000 British military personnel took part in 45 large-scale tests in Australia and the South Pacific. Many later suffered cancers and blood disorders that they believe were caused by radiation exposure. For decades the Ministry of Defence denied that blood tests had been carried out on personnel, but a 2024 investigation found that medical examinations and blood testing had taken place across all three services, and that some records were missing or had been destroyed. In September, the former defence minister Tobias Ellwood said he had unwittingly misled Parliament in 2018 about the records. The Guardian reported that Burnham has described the matter as a "criminal cover-up on an industrial scale".

On BBC Newsnight, the veteran John Morris, who developed cancer and lost a four-month-old son, said he hoped for justice 72 years after being sent to Christmas Island. The Mirror, which has campaigned on the issue for four decades, welcomed the panel, and Ellwood wrote in the Daily Mail that the scandal was as big as the Post Office affair.

## 6. A US jury convicts Huawei of conspiring to steal trade secrets, bank fraud and violating sanctions on Iran, but acquits the Chinese company of racketeering, the most serious charge it faced

A federal jury in Brooklyn has found Huawei guilty of 10 of the 12 criminal counts it faced, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud, wire fraud and conspiracy to violate US sanctions, after a trial lasting about two months. The jurors acquitted the Chinese technology giant of racketeering conspiracy, the case's most serious and overarching charge, which required them to find the company acted like a criminal enterprise, and of conspiracy to obstruct justice. The verdicts also covered three Huawei subsidiaries.

Prosecutors accused Huawei of stealing technology from US companies, deceiving banks about its business in Iran and, according to NBC News, helping Iran spy on its own citizens. "For decades, Huawei and three of its subsidiaries conducted their business in the United States on the basis of lies and theft," said the US Attorney General, Todd Blanche. Huawei said the racketeering acquittal "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis", and its lawyer Brian Heberlig said the company expected to appeal. Sentencing is planned for September 2027, when Huawei could face fines, forfeiture and restitution that DW said could run to billions of dollars.

The prosecution dates back to 2018, when Huawei's chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou, was detained in Canada on a US warrant, triggering a diplomatic crisis involving the US, China and Canada. She returned to China three years later under a deal with US prosecutors and the charges against her were dropped. Huawei has since been barred from selling networking equipment to US carriers, though its consumer business has kept growing.
