## 3. A long-awaited review will call for a 'fundamental overhaul' of Pip disability benefit assessments, with a fast track for the most severely ill, ahead of its publication on Tuesday

The disability minister, Sir Stephen Timms, says his review of the personal independence payment (Pip) will call for a "fundamental overhaul" of how claims are assessed so the system can "better recognise people's varying costs and needs". Writing in the Times ahead of the full report's publication on Tuesday, he said the current set-up was "no longer fit for purpose" after "marked shifts in the nature of disability and ill health" in the 13 years since Pip was created, including substantial growth in claims for mental health and neurodiverse conditions.

He said people with the most severe conditions and those receiving end-of-life care should have a "fast track" that is "less burdensome", and that someone with a lifelong or degenerative illness "should not need regular reassessments which feel adversarial and unnecessary".

The review was commissioned after the previous government under Sir Keir Starmer abandoned planned cuts to disability and sickness benefits in 2025 in the face of a rebellion by more than 130 MPs. It comes a week after Alan Milburn's review of young people not in work or education, whose recommendation to move 18 to 24-year-olds off Pip onto a new system the government has accepted. The BBC reports that Pip costs are expected to rise above £41bn by 2030 and that about four million people were claiming it in April. The Telegraph says Burnham risks a major rebellion from Labour MPs if he revisits eligibility and cost, while the Guardian reports the review will favour overhaul rather than scrapping the benefit for under-25s.

## 4. Nearly 700,000 women across the UK are to be invited to join the world's largest trial of whether artificial intelligence can help spot breast cancer earlier in NHS screening

Nearly 700,000 women across the UK are to be invited to take part in the world's largest trial of artificial intelligence in breast cancer screening. The EDITH trial will use AI to review mammograms to see whether it can improve early detection and ease pressure on clinical staff without over-diagnosing women unnecessarily. Recruitment has begun at Cambridge University Hospitals, the first of 30 sites.

At present two NHS specialists look at each mammogram. In the trial, one of those human readers will be replaced by AI. Women aged 50 to 71 are invited for routine screening every three years and more than two million attend each year. Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in the UK, with around 59,000 cases a year, about a third of them found through routine mammograms, and the screening programme is estimated to prevent around 1,300 deaths a year.

Smaller studies have suggested AI can improve detection rates, but the researchers say they need to be sure it does not cause harm by recalling women unnecessarily. "We want to avoid the overdiagnosis of milder disease that would never have caused symptoms," said co-chief investigator Professor Sian Taylor-Phillips of the University of Warwick. Professor Fiona Gilbert of the University of Cambridge said fewer than 1% of women screened have cancer, and that technology could free radiologists to spend more time with patients. She told the BBC there was a "huge shortfall" in radiologists.

## 5. Dozens of hospital trusts in England warn that NHS savings demands threaten their ability to function, as some plan 'unpalatable' cuts to services and staff

Dozens of hospitals in England are so short of money that they may struggle to keep caring for patients normally and are being forced into "unpalatable" cuts to services and staff, the Guardian reports. Thirty-one NHS trusts have recorded in their published risk registers that the savings they have been told to make this year threaten their ability to function, and 27 of them rate lack of money as the biggest threat they face.

Six trusts have given that danger a rating of 25, the highest possible score. They are King's College Hospital in London, which is seeking to save £85m this year, the St George's, Epsom and St Helier group, which has a target of £91m, the North Midlands, Gloucestershire and Doncaster and Bassetlaw acute trusts, and Birmingham and Solihull mental health trust. Many more trusts have launched programmes to save as much as £106m each by limiting services and reducing their workforce, despite widespread understaffing.

Trust bosses describe their finances as "precarious" and "severe". They blame rising drug prices, strikes, the cost of covering pay rises, extra demand during this summer's heatwaves and the introduction of electronic health records. Privately, the paper says, they accuse NHS England of imposing unreasonable targets so that the health service as a whole can balance its books after hospitals were told to stop overspending. The report comes as the Guardian separately says patients may miss out on weight-loss jabs after GP funding was withdrawn.

## 6. The longest UN climate summit on record ends in Turkey with no deal, after the EU and Pacific nations reject a final text that made no mention of fossil fuels

The Cop31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, ended on Sunday without a major new agreement, after running two days past their scheduled close to become the longest UN climate conference in history. The final draft, put forward by hosts Turkey and Australia, was rejected by the European Union, Pacific island nations and others because the words "fossil fuels" did not appear anywhere in it. Many poorer countries also objected to the absence of clear financial commitments from richer nations to help them adapt.

The BBC says the last time a Cop's main political agreement collapsed in this way was in Copenhagen in 2009. The EU's representative, the Irish minister Darragh O'Brien, said it would be "unconscionable" to have a climate pact that did not mention fossil fuels. Turkey's Cop31 president Murat Kurum told the closing plenary there would be no new text: "There is nothing to do, we did all that we could."

UN Secretary-General António Guterres, attending his last Cop, said he was "deeply disappointed" and accused "powerful fossil fuel interests" of taking "a page from Big Tobacco's playbook". Australia's prime minister Anthony Albanese, whose country co-hosted the summit, blamed "difficult geopolitical times". CBS said delegations from nearly 200 countries took part, and CGTN noted that expectations had been low as countries grappled with an oil price shock and tensions stemming from the war in Iran.

## 7. A Russian navy corvette sinks at Libya's Benghazi naval base after a suspected Ukrainian sea drone attack, more than 1,000 miles from southern Ukraine

A Russian warship docked at the Benghazi naval base in eastern Libya sank after an explosion in the early hours of Sunday that Libyan officials blamed on a suspected drone attack. The Guardian, citing officials from Libya's eastern government who spoke to the Associated Press, said the vessel was the Russian navy corvette Merkury, which had stopped to refuel and take on supplies on its way back to Russia. Officials said at least four drones were intercepted and two struck the ship. Satellite images showed it capsized and largely submerged, with only the bow visible.

The eastern-based Government of National Stability said there were no casualties, condemned the attack as a violation of Libyan sovereignty and said it was working with Russian officials to establish who was responsible. Moscow had not commented. Later on Sunday, Libya's health ministry said people with minor injuries had been brought in from a second, commercial vessel targeted off Benghazi. The UN mission in Libya called for an "immediate, independent and transparent investigation", warning that the country "must not become a battleground for foreign conflicts".

Ukraine has not confirmed involvement. Jalel Harchaoui of the Royal United Services Institute told the BBC that "Ukraine is the only actor brazen enough to pull something like that off." The Mirror said two Libyan military officials suggested the drones had been launched from a base operated by Kyiv's forces in western Libya, a claim that has not been independently confirmed.
