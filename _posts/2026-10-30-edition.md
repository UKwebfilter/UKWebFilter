## 1. Sir Patrick Stewart, the Yorkshire-born star of Star Trek, X-Men and the Royal Shakespeare Company, dies aged 86

Sir Patrick Stewart, the stage and screen actor best known as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation and Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men films, has died aged 86. His longtime spokesperson, Kelly Bush Novak, said he died at his home in Los Angeles on Wednesday after "a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia", The Guardian and CBS News reported.

The BBC said Stewart was born in Mirfield in the West Riding of Yorkshire in 1940, left school at 15 to work as a reporter on the Dewsbury Reporter, and went on to the Bristol Old Vic and the Royal Shakespeare Company. He was cast as Picard at 47, playing the role over seven television seasons, four films and the later series Star Trek: Picard. He will be seen again as Xavier in Avengers: Doomsday in December.

Sir Ian McKellen called him "the most respected and beloved actor of his generation on either side of the Atlantic", and Hugh Jackman, William Shatner and George Takei also paid tribute. In a career spanning seven decades he received a knighthood and a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame, the BBC said.

## 2. Healey backs away from cutting the welfare bill before the next election, hours after saying there would be a reduction

Chancellor John Healey backed away from cutting the cost of welfare in this parliament, a day after his first Budget, The Guardian reported. Asked on BBC Radio 4's Today programme whether the benefits bill would fall before the next election, he said: "There will be. There will be a reduction in the benefits bill." Treasury officials later said he had not meant to give a timeline, and he told Channel 4: "It's not correct to say that we are going to bring this welfare bill down in this parliament because we've not set a timetable for that."

The Guardian said the Institute for Fiscal Studies warned Healey would have needed an extra £28bn a year to cover inflation and a 3% of GDP defence target by 2030, and that the coming spending review "looks pretty brutal for some departments". The Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts welfare spending rising from £334bn last year to £431bn by 2030. Healey pointed instead to coming reviews by Alan Milburn and Stephen Timms. The Daily Mail called it a "screeching U-turn", while the Independent said No 10 was forced to admit welfare would not be cut by the next election.

## 4. The online "manosphere" should be treated as a public health problem like smoking and fast food, a report warns

The online "manosphere" should be treated as a public health problem with regulations similar to those on smoking and fast food, according to a new report covered by the Daily Mirror, Sky News, The Guardian and others. The authors argue that online platforms and companies profit from young men's anger and insecurities, and that causing outrage is seen as "good for business".

The report describes the manosphere as a money-making "loose collection" of online communities "united by shared beliefs about masculinity, misogyny and male victimisation", and links it to health harms including violence against women and girls and mental health problems, as well as "delegitimising" the idea of men and boys seeking help, the Mirror said. The authors call for a coordinated response from governments, schools, health services and community groups, and for "harnessing the power of algorithms" to promote positive messages. Co-author Professor Sarah Hawkes of the Global 50/50 think tank said the manosphere offers boys "belonging, identity and an explanation for why life feels hard", adding: "We need to offer boys something better, not just tell them what to avoid." The Times reported the authors' comparison with the tobacco industry.

## 6. Crypto exchange Binance says $6.3bn of assets were hit in what may be the largest hack in history, with analysts blaming North Korea

Binance, the world's biggest cryptocurrency exchange, said on Thursday that $6.3bn (£4.7bn) of digital assets had been affected by what could be the largest hack in history, the BBC reported. It said about half had been frozen with the help of other platforms and that it would "cover all losses". Early checks suggested an engineer's compromised laptop allowed an attacker to make unauthorised withdrawals.

Two analytics firms, TRM Labs and Chainalysis, attributed the attack to a North Korean hacking unit called TraderTraitor, the BBC said. Binance did not respond directly on the North Korea link but said it was working with law enforcement "to bring them to justice". Tether and Circle said they had frozen about $2.4bn of assets between them, and withdrawals were suspended. The Guardian said Binance had located about $3bn so far, with no update on the remaining $3.3bn, and that bitcoin fell more than 3% to a six-week low of $76,368 in early Asian trading on Friday, while shares in Coinbase slid 4.8%. Binance has about 335 million registered users. The incident eclipses the $1.5bn Bybit hack in 2025.
