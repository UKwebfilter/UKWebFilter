## 1. Chancellor John Healey rules out raising capital gains tax in his first Budget

Chancellor John Healey has dismissed calls from some Labour MPs to increase capital gains tax (CGT) when he delivers his first Budget, due on 28 October, the BBC and The Times reported. CGT is charged on the profit people make when they sell an asset that has risen in value, such as shares or properties that are not a main home.

The Times said Healey rejected an increase amid concerns that it would damage economic growth and hinder investment, and reported warnings that it could end up costing the government money if wealthy people leave the country or put off selling assets. The paper quoted him saying he wanted a "steady as she goes" Budget, and said a tax raid on banks is expected to be the biggest revenue raiser. Cabinet ministers Wes Streeting and Louise Haigh had both previously voiced support for raising CGT, while Lord Jim O'Neill, a former adviser to Andy Burnham, warned a rise would send "the wrong message to business".

Other papers looked at what the Budget may contain instead. The Telegraph reported that Healey plans a Budget raid on family businesses and that £2bn has been cut from the Army's frontline equipment as the chancellor tries to fill a hole in defence spending. The Daily Mail led on Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch's warning that the Budget could be even worse than those delivered by Rachel Reeves.

## 2. Drivers get grace periods and a £20 cap for honest mistakes under new private parking rules

Drivers will get new rights to challenge "rip-off" private parking charges under a code of practice set out by Prime Minister Andy Burnham, covering England, Scotland and Wales. Motorists will have a guaranteed five minutes to decide whether to park and 10 minutes to leave after their session ends before facing a penalty. Charges are to be cancelled for problems drivers cannot control, such as breakdowns in car parks, and for honest mistakes including stopping to check directions, medical emergencies or broken ticket machines. Slip-ups such as entering the wrong registration will carry a maximum charge of £20 on appeal, and the maximum parking charge will be frozen at £100.

The BBC reported that operators who do not comply risk losing access to DVLA data, and The Times said firms that break the rules would lose the power to pursue drivers through the courts. Burnham said being "a few minutes late back to your car or typing one digit of your numberplate wrong shouldn't end with a hefty fine". The RAC has said a record 16.9 million private parking tickets were issued in 2025-26, up 17% in a year.

The AA said drivers "finally have something" to protect them, while the RAC called for debt recovery fees to be banned and for a single independent appeals body. The consumer group Which? told the Guardian the rules fell well short of what was needed, and the British Parking Association said many of the principles were already in the existing voluntary code.

## 3. Killers and serious sex offenders detained in hospital to lose benefits under new rules

Killers and serious sex offenders who are detained in hospital rather than prison will no longer receive benefits under regulations due to be laid before Parliament on Tuesday. The change ends what the government calls "double provision", under which serious offenders could receive up to £800 a month in universal credit while their bed and board are already funded through the NHS during psychiatric treatment. Prisoners are already banned from claiming state benefits, but offenders treated in psychiatric hospitals have remained eligible, the Daily Mail reported.

The government said closing the loophole would save an estimated tens of millions of pounds a year, and the change is expected to affect about 2,500 offenders already in hospital. The rules are expected to come into force early next year, subject to parliamentary approval, and cover offences including manslaughter, attempted murder, rape and wounding with intent to cause grievous bodily harm.

The move follows a campaign by the families of Barnaby Webber, Grace O'Malley-Kumar and Ian Coates, who were killed by Valdo Calocane in Nottingham in 2023. Barnaby's mother, Emma Webber, called it "long overdue, and simply the right thing to do". Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden said violent criminals had had their living costs paid by the taxpayer "twice over". Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately said Labour had taken all year to close a loophole it had promised to close.

## 4. Energy secretary becomes latest cabinet minister to question Mahmood's settlement reforms

Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh has become the latest cabinet minister to criticise Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood's plans to make it harder for foreign workers to win permanent residence in the UK, The Times reported. In a write-up of a community meeting in her Peckham constituency in September, first highlighted by The Telegraph, Fahnbulleh was reported as saying: "If we continually prolong the time people have to wait for settlement, we can also prolong the process of building cohesion."

The Guardian reported that the post has since been removed, with her office saying the remarks had been paraphrased and were not a true representation of her views. The government has proposed increasing the standard qualifying period for indefinite leave to remain from five years to 10, though it is likely to make concessions for some professions, including care workers, and the route to settlement for refugees would be extended to up to 20 years. A final response to the consultation has not yet been published, though Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is committed to the bulk of the changes.

The Daily Mail described "new divisions" in Burnham's cabinet and noted that Angela Rayner had previously called the plans "un-British" and "wrong". The Guardian said Rayner warned last month that the changes, if left unamended, would unfairly penalise care staff. Fahnbulleh came to the UK as a child after fleeing the civil war in Liberia.

## 6. France imposes curfews in Paris and other cities as minister admits police lost control

French authorities have imposed overnight curfews in parts of central Paris and other cities after a weekend of rioting and looting across the country. Paris police said a curfew would run from 23:00 to 06:00 local time in Place de la République and the Les Halles shopping area, and unauthorised gatherings were banned in other parts of the city centre. Partial curfews restricting gatherings and the movement of young people were announced for cities including Marseille, Bordeaux, Nice, Lyon and Lille, the BBC reported.

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said mayors would be given the power to impose curfews "where they need", did not rule out a state of emergency and announced what CGTN described as a "large-scale judicial operation" against those behind the violence. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez admitted police had "lost control" in some cities, and President Emmanuel Macron refused his resignation. More than 2,500 people have been arrested and about 1,000 police officers injured, officials said. The protests began last month with students demanding better school conditions and widened into anger over fuel prices, the cost of living and public services, with about 550,000 people demonstrating at the weekend.

The head of France's chambers of commerce said damage was estimated at "hundreds of millions of euros". DW reported that US President Donald Trump cited the unrest at a Kansas rally, describing France as "conquered, looted, and burned to the ground by migrants, jihadists and communists". The US, China and Germany have issued travel warnings to their citizens.
