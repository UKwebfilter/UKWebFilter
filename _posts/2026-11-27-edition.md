## 1. Nearly 100 Labour MPs, including a handful of frontbenchers, write to Andy Burnham urging him not to approve the Rosebank oilfield, exposing the deepest party split of his premiership so far

Labour's divisions over the giant Rosebank oilfield have broken into the open after 92 backbenchers and at least four frontbenchers signed a letter to Andy Burnham urging him not to let his ministers approve the project, the biggest undeveloped field in UK waters, the Guardian reported. The chancellor, John Healey, and many Scottish Labour MPs are pushing for Rosebank to go ahead, while dozens of others want it rejected on climate grounds. The Guardian said more than one frontbench ministerial aide was considering resigning if it is approved.

Catherine Fookes, the Monmouthshire MP who helped organise the letter, said concern was "widespread within the parliamentary Labour party" and that others shared it without signing. Rosebank "will do nothing to reduce energy bills for our constituents", she wrote, arguing that the oil belongs to private companies that will sell it to the highest bidder. The Times quoted a frontbencher who said tackling climate change was some MPs' "number one reason for being Labour", while a supporter of the project said "the anti-crowd are resorting to internal party politics".

Labour's 2024 manifesto promised no new North Sea licences, but ministers approved the smaller Jackdaw gasfield last month. Both fields had to resubmit their environmental applications after a court ruling last year that companies must account for emissions from burning the oil and gas they produce. The Conservatives have promised to scrap the Climate Change Act, and Reform UK says it would cancel all government green spending.

## 2. Net migration to the UK falls to 77,000 in the year to June, its lowest level since 1997 outside the pandemic, as ministers claim credit and the Conservatives point to the number of Britons leaving

Net migration to the UK fell to an estimated 77,000 in the year to June 2026, the lowest 12-month figure since 1997 if the Covid pandemic years are excluded, according to figures from the Office for National Statistics and the Home Office. About 738,000 people moved to the UK and 661,000 left. The total was down from 204,000 in the previous 12 months and from a peak of 944,000 in the year to March 2023. It is the first time net migration has been in the "tens of thousands" since David Cameron's government set that target in 2010.

The Guardian reported that immigration fell by 17% over the year, driven by fewer non-EU citizens being given permission to work, study, join family or claim asylum, while emigration fell by 4%. Visa grants to international students dropped by 38% in the first half of 2026. The Daily Mail highlighted that 245,000 of those leaving were British nationals, including 126,000 aged 16 to 34.

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood said "this government has restored control" after the Conservatives "lost control of our borders". Andy Burnham said the country was "finally... getting into the position that the public has long wanted to see", and rejected the idea that Brexit had helped. Kemi Badenoch said Conservative changes in 2023 had brought the numbers down, and told Sky News that "a lot of good people are leaving this country" because of the government's approach to business.

The Daily Mail reported that four of the attempted murders took place at locations between Peterborough and London before the attack on the train.

Passengers fought Williams off with a frying pan and a toaster as he moved through the carriages, the BBC reported. LNER crew member Samir Zitouni, 48, built what the court heard was a "defensive blockade" and suffered life-threatening injuries. Stephen Crean, 61, who was returning from a football match, was stabbed several times after confronting Williams. A student who escaped told the BBC she owed Mr Crean her life; the two met for the first time after the verdicts.

The Mail reported that Williams had been diagnosed with schizophrenia long before the attacks and had stopped taking his medication days earlier, but psychiatrists for both sides agreed he had retained the capacity to form intent. Two Cambridgeshire police constables face a gross misconduct hearing over an earlier allegation that Williams had injured a man with a knife, and NHS England said it would hold an independent investigation into his healthcare. The Mail said he would remain at Rampton high-security hospital.

## 4. The Ministry of Defence admits it has email addresses for only about half its strategic reserve, as Defence Secretary Wes Streeting confirms letters asking veterans to update their details and skills

The Ministry of Defence is urging former service personnel in its strategic reserve to update their contact details after it emerged that it does not have email addresses for about half of them, the BBC reported. The reserve is estimated at 95,000 veterans who can be recalled in exceptional circumstances, but the MoD said it had only been able to send letters to around 50,000, and has published open letters on its website asking others to "reconnect".

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting confirmed on Thursday that the ministry had written to ex-military personnel to check their details and skills against a rising "threat environment", after The Times and the Telegraph reported the letters. "I want the country to be alert, not alarmed," he told BBC Radio 4's Today programme. The MoD said the threshold for recall was high, that reservists would not be used to fill routine staffing gaps in peacetime, and that they would be invited to volunteer for exercises including NATO's Steadfast Defender next spring.

Fox News, citing the Telegraph, said it was the first time since the Cold War that the ministry had contacted reservists on such a scale, and noted a warning from the International Institute for Strategic Studies that suspected Russian sabotage in Europe could cause a "mass-casualty event". Former defence secretary Lord Robertson had warned in April that the MoD "doesn't even know where most of them are". RT said Moscow had repeatedly dismissed claims it intends to attack NATO.

## 6. Two Italian technicians working for Rheinmetall are killed in a drone strike on a military site in Kuwait that Kuwait and Italy blame on Iran, while Tehran denies involvement and calls the attack suspicious

Armed forces spokesman Abolfazl Shekarchi said "no military or offensive operations have been conducted", and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the incident "suspicious", offering to work with Kuwait and Italy to determine the cause, the BBC reported. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni expressed her condolences, and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called for more pressure on Iran to end "unacceptable attacks" on its neighbours, DW reported. The Gulf Cooperation Council called the attack "an extremely dangerous and unacceptable escalation". Kuwait, which hosts US forces, has been repeatedly targeted since the US and Israel went to war with Iran in February.

## 7. A powerful Thanksgiving storm leaves close to a million US homes and businesses without power, floods coastal towns and disrupts thousands of flights as New Jersey and New York declare emergencies

A powerful storm battered the eastern United States on Thanksgiving Day with winds of up to 70mph, torrential rain and coastal flooding, leaving almost a million households without power by Thursday night, the BBC reported. Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia and New Jersey were among the hardest hit. Outage figures rose through the day: CGTN, citing data from mid-afternoon, put the number above 800,000, while Al Jazeera reported more than 900,000 homes and businesses in the dark by evening.
